## Abstract

Do tornadoes, severe hail, and heat waves occur at different rates during concurrent drought-pluvial event (CDPE) months over the contiguous United States? The headline catalog is the companion national study's catalog, built with its frozen detector on SPEIbase v2.11: 52 CDPE months from the 1-month standardized precipitation evapotranspiration index (SPEI-1) over January 1980 to December 2023, with a 15-month US-anchored subset whose poles both fall inside the contiguous United States. A negative binomial count model with calendar month effects, a year term, and an Oceanic Niño Index covariate is primary, with a calendar-stratified ratio of means retained for continuity. A lagged arm relating CDPE months to the following month's hazard counts addresses reverse causation in the monthly index. Same-month incidence rate ratios are 1.09 (0.79 to 1.49) for tornadoes, 1.12 (0.89 to 1.41) for severe hail, and 0.91 (0.60 to 1.37) for heat-wave days. Lagged estimates are 1.00, 0.96, and 1.14, with month-paired arm differences including zero for all three hazards. A same-month pole-relative pattern (tornadoes near the wet pole) does not survive lagging and is exploratory. The design detects only rate ratios above about 1.6 to 1.9, so the evidence is inconclusive rather than a proven null.

**Keywords:** compound events, concurrent drought-pluvial events, SPEI, tornadoes, heat waves, sensitivity analysis

# 1. Introduction

Compound and concurrent extremes cause impacts that single-hazard analyses miss [@zscheischler2020]. One such pattern is the concurrent drought-pluvial event (CDPE), a month in which one part of a domain is anomalously dry and another part is anomalously wet, a configuration the literature classifies as spatially compounding [@zscheischler2020; @zscheischler2017]. Related work has approached the drought-pluvial relationship through event coincidence at a point [@hesheffield2020], through network representations of drought-pluvial dipoles [@wanghe2025], through spatially compounding drought-flood events over Europe [@brunner2025], and through temporal dipoles within subregions of the Great Plains [@christian2015]. The dedicated CDPE literature remains limited worldwide, a point a review of compound drought and pluvial extremes in the Canadian Prairies also supports [@wheaton2023], and general reviews of compound hazards make the same point at broader scope [@aghakouchak2020].

This paper asks a frequency question. Do tornadoes, severe hail, and heat waves occur at different rates during CDPE regime months than during comparable non-CDPE months? The question is worth asking because large-scale climate states modulate severe convective weather: the El Niño-Southern Oscillation shifts tornado and hail frequency over the United States [@allentippett2015], and modeled environments link large hail to the large-scale state [@battaglioli2023]. Heat waves, whose measurement conventions are themselves a methodological literature [@perkins2013; @domeisen2023], are the natural hazard of the dry side of the dipole, and work on land-atmosphere coupling in the 2022 compound drought-heatwave events over the contiguous United States (CONUS) shows one route by which dry conditions and heat reinforce each other [@yoon2024]. Hail climatologies carry their own reporting complications [@raupach2021; @allentippett2021], and geographic modeling of the likelihood of extreme hail sizes shows how strongly hail outcomes vary in space [@das2024]. To our knowledge, no earlier study has conditioned the occurrence of these three hazards on CDPE regime months, and we make that scoped claim only for the CDPE conditioning itself.

One hypothesis motivates the design without being assumed by it. A CDPE month may be a large-scale circulation regime, with a ridge over the dry pole and a trough near the wet pole, and such a regime would plausibly redistribute more than precipitation. This paper performs no composite analysis, so that circulation picture is an open hypothesis here, not a premise, and nothing in the analysis depends on it. What the analysis does depend on is the catalog, and every CDPE catalog is the output of a chain of analyst choices: the index product and its release, the accumulation timescale, the fitting distribution, the calibration period, the treatment of evaporative demand, the detection threshold and buffer radius, and whether computed fields are masked to land. For single-index drought assessment, several of these choices move classifications materially [@stagge2015; @begueria2014; @sheffield2012]. For CDPEs the problem compounds, because an event requires a dry and a wet classification to hold at once in different places. A hazard claim built on a single catalog therefore inherits the catalog choices, and this paper reports its frequency results beside a definition envelope and sensitivity arms that show how much those choices matter.

The paper is the frequency companion to a national catalog study. Its headline catalog is that companion study's catalog, the Study-1 catalog built on SPEIbase v2.11, so the two studies share one catalog rather than describing similar ones (Sokol 2026, under review). The contributions are threefold. We estimate hazard occurrence in CDPE months on the shared catalog with a count model as the primary estimand. We add a lagged arm that separates the association from the reverse-causation channel built into a monthly index. And we report the estimates beside their definition envelope, their pole geography, and their detectability limits, so that the strength of the evidence is part of the result. Section 2 describes the detector and its provenance, the hazard data, the estimands, and the sensitivity arms. Section 3 reports the aligned catalog and its geography, the headline conditioning, the lagged arm, robustness displays, pole-relative strata, the envelope, and detectability. Section 4 discusses what the conditioning does and does not show, and Section 5 concludes.

# 2. Data and methods

## 2.1 CDPE detection and catalog provenance

We detect CDPEs with the frozen detector of the companion national study, implemented in NumPy for this study and validated against the original code, which returns identical month sets on common inputs. For each month and standardized index field over the CONUS bounding box (25 to 50 N, 125 to 66 W), the detector (1) locates the driest and wettest cells, (2) draws a circular buffer of radius r around each, and (3) qualifies a buffer when fewer than a fraction q of its cells are missing and at least a fraction f of its non-missing cells pass the threshold theta in the appropriate direction. A month is a CDPE month only if both buffers qualify, the both-pole gate. We classify months independently, with no duration or merging rule. The baseline operating point is SPEI-1 with theta = 1.5, r = 4.0 degrees, f = 0.5, and q = 0.10. The standardized precipitation evapotranspiration index (SPEI) combines a climatic water balance with a standardized fit [@vicenteserrano2010; @begueria2014], and the standardized precipitation index (SPI) applies the same standardization to precipitation alone [@mckee1993].

The baseline index is the 1-month SPEI, a monthly water-balance anomaly. It is not a multi-month accumulation, and we do not describe the baseline catalog as a catalog of sustained or multi-month drought. Longer accumulations enter only through the definition envelope, which adds the 3-month and 6-month SPEI (SPEI-3 and SPEI-6) as alternative definitions. Because catalogs built by different parts of this study rest on different inputs, Table 1 gives the provenance of every analysis set, and the text states which set each result uses. The headline catalog is the Study-1 catalog on SPEIbase v2.11. The definition envelope runs on the staged SPEIbase v2.10 fields, because SPEIbase v2.11 files for SPEI-3 and SPEI-6 could not be obtained, so the envelope is a sensitivity ensemble over definitions on the stated v2.10 release while the headline catalog is the catalog of record on v2.11. The Arm A SPI-1 sets were computed from CRU TS 4.09 precipitation [@harris2020] with the climate_indices package [@adams2017] under a gamma fit and a 1981 to 2010 calibration, while the Arm A SPEI fields rest on CRU TS 4.08 through SPEIbase v2.10. We disclose that mixture of CRU versions explicitly rather than presenting the Arm A inputs as a single release.

The US-anchored subset carries a second headline conditioning, because a large share of the aligned catalog poles fall outside the United States (Section 3.1).

## 2.2 Hazard data

We take tornado and hail occurrence from the NOAA Storm Events database [@ncei_storm_events], yearly details files, January 1980 to December 2023. We assign events to the study domain (25 to 50 N, 125 to 66 W) by their begin coordinates, with county centroids from the Census Bureau gazetteer as the fallback for records lacking coordinates (of 434,420 tornado and hail records processed, 413,052 carried usable begin coordinates, we placed 21,178 by county centroid, 190 could not be located and were excluded, and 193 located records fell outside the domain). The primary tornado metric is the monthly count of tornado reports. Pre-specified secondary metrics restrict the count to tornadoes rated F/EF1 or stronger and F/EF2 or stronger. The primary hail metric is the monthly count of hail reports of at least 1.00 inch, with all hail reports as the secondary metric. The NWS severe-hail threshold was 0.75 inch until 5 January 2010 and 1.00 inch thereafter, and this study applies 1.00 inch throughout the record for consistency rather than following the threshold in force at the time. Storm Events reporting is known to be inhomogeneous: counts rose with population, spotter networks, and the deployment of the NEXRAD radar network, weak-tornado reporting is the least homogeneous component [@verbout2006], and hail report characteristics changed over the record [@allentippett2021]. The reporting step of 1996 to 1998 is visible in the monthly hail series (Section 3.4). We therefore make no trend claims, and we report a split-era comparison (1980 to 1995 against 1996 to 2023) as a descriptive robustness display.

We compute heat waves from gridMET daily maximum temperature [@abatzoglou2013], aggregated to a 0.5 degree grid by cell-mean binning. Cells valid on at least 99 percent of days in 1981 to 2010 form the land set (3,608 of the 5,900 cells). A cell heat-wave day is a day in a run of at least three consecutive days above the cell calendar-day 98th percentile, computed over 1981 to 2010 within a 31-day window, following established percentile-based practice [@perkins2013]. The monthly metric is total heat-wave cell-days divided by the number of land cells, interpretable as mean heat-wave days per cell, and the count models below use the underlying cell-day counts.

## 2.3 Estimands

The primary estimand is the coefficient on a CDPE-month indicator in a negative binomial (NB) count model of the monthly hazard count, with calendar month fixed effects, a linear year term, and the Oceanic Niño Index (ONI) as a covariate. The ONI series is the NOAA Climate Prediction Center series distributed by the NOAA Physical Sciences Laboratory, centered 3-month running mean of ERSST v6 sea surface temperature anomalies, retrieved on 2 November 2026 and archived with the analysis code. The exponentiated CDPE coefficient is an incidence rate ratio (IRR). Primary intervals are cluster-robust by year, and year-block bootstrap intervals (B = 2000) are reported alongside in the archived results. We fitted Poisson models first, and the Pearson dispersion ranged from 44.9 to 2048 across hazards and arms, which rules the Poisson variance out and makes the NB model primary in every cell. Poisson estimates are reported in the archived results for comparison.

**Lagged design.** The lagged arm repeats both estimators with the exposure shifted one month: the antecedent-month CDPE sets are the aligned catalog and its US-anchored subset shifted by one month, and the outcomes are hazard counts in the following month, over February 1980 to December 2023. The rationale is circularity. Same-month SPEI-1 is built partly from the precipitation of the month itself, including precipitation the storms themselves produce, so a same-month association between CDPE status and convective counts can be partly reverse-caused. The lagged arm cannot be reverse-caused in that way, because the index month precedes the outcome month, and a comparison of the two arms is therefore informative about how much of a same-month estimate the circularity channel can explain. The arms are also compared directly, by a month-paired bootstrap that resamples outcome months, refits both count models on each resample with the negative binomial dispersion held at its full-frame estimate for the hazard and arm, and takes the difference in log incidence rate ratio per replicate (B = 2000).

For continuity with the earlier analysis of this catalog family, we also compute the calendar-stratified ratio of means. Calendar months form 12 strata. Within each stratum, we compare the mean monthly hazard metric in CDPE months with the mean in non-CDPE months of the same calendar month, and we average the stratum ratios with weights equal to the share of CDPE months falling in the stratum. Calendar matching absorbs seasonality and month length by construction, and intervals come from a whole-year bootstrap (B = 2000) that recomputes the ratio per replicate [@kunsch1989].

The robustness set is fixed in advance of interpretation. We repeat both arms with the April and May 2011 CDPE months excluded (the 2011 outbreak cluster), we compute leave-one-month-out ranges for the ratio estimator, we compute median contrasts between CDPE and non-CDPE months (reported in the Supplementary Information), and we compute a split-era display in which each era carries its own within-era rate ratio and the era difference, the ratio of the later-era ratio to the earlier-era ratio, carries its own bootstrap interval. The pole-relative analysis classifies each 1-degree report cell of each CDPE month by its distance to that month poles: cells within 4.0 degrees of a pole form the near-dry and near-wet strata, cells within 4.0 degrees of both poles are assigned to the nearer pole, and all remaining cells form the elsewhere stratum. Controls are the same calendar months of non-CDPE years, classified by the CDPE month poles, and intervals come from a bootstrap that resamples CDPE months (B = 2000). The pole-relative analysis is also run lagged: the following month's reports are classified by the regime month poles, with controls drawn from the following months of matched non-CDPE regime months. Heat waves are excluded from the pole-relative analysis, because the heat-wave field is a gridMET mean over its own land grid rather than report counts on the 1-degree report grid. Detectability is summarized by an analytic minimum detectable effect size (MDES), the smallest rate ratio detectable at 80 percent power in a two-sided test at alpha = 0.05 under the ratio-of-means design, computed from the observed group means and delta-method log-count variances, with the protective-side MDES the reciprocal. We also compute the Spearman correlation between annual CDPE month counts and annual hazard totals over the 44 years.

## 2.4 Sensitivity arms

Arm A is the definition envelope. It takes precomputed index products, SPEI at 1-, 3-, and 6-month timescales from SPEIbase v2.10 [@begueria2014; @harris2020] and the 1-month SPI described in Section 2.1, and varies only the detection threshold theta in {1.0, 1.5, 2.0} and the radius r in {3, 4, 5} degrees, giving 36 configurations over January 1980 to December 2023. Hazard envelopes use the configurations with at least 10 CDPE months, 23 of the 36. The configurations are nested: they share months with each other and they all condition the same hazard series, so they are not independent tests, and envelope agreement is agreement within one nested family rather than repeated confirmation. We summarize catalog sensitivity with an exact variance decomposition of CDPE month counts and of log counts for the balanced design [@saltelli2008], and we measure agreement between configurations with the Jaccard index of month sets [@jaccard1912] reported beside its chance expectation.

Arm B, reported in full in the Supplementary Information, refits standardized indices from North American Regional Reanalysis (NARR) [@mesinger2006] and ERA5 [@hersbach2020] fields over 1980 to 2014 in 720 configurations that cross dataset, timescale, fitting distribution (three-parameter log-logistic by probability-weighted moments [@vicenteserrano2010; @singh1993], or an empirical transform with the Gringorten plotting position [@gringorten1963]), calibration period, water balance (SPEI with Thornthwaite evapotranspiration [@thornthwaite1948; @allen1998], or precipitation only), threshold, and radius. Its primary form applies a land mask derived from SPEIbase to every computed field before detection. Section 3.6 summarizes the corrected Arm B results, and the Supplementary Information gives the corrected tie-break rule, the corrected baseline counts, and the corrected variance decomposition.

# 3. Results

## 3.1 The aligned catalog and pole geography

The aligned catalog holds 52 CDPE months, all inside January 1980 to December 2023, with the latest month in April 2022 and no month in 2024. The earlier Arm A port returned 46 months at the same baseline settings, and the relationship between the two catalogs is now established rather than conjectured. The port is faithful on both fields: run on the SPEIbase v2.11 field it returns exactly the 52 aligned months, and run on the staged v2.10 field it returns the 46 Arm A months. The catalogs differ because the SPEIbase releases differ (v2.10 on CRU TS 4.08 against v2.11 on CRU TS 4.09), and the month-level difference is symmetric: the intersection is 32 months, 14 months appear only in Arm A, and 20 months appear only in the aligned catalog, for a Jaccard index of 0.485 against 4.5 shared months expected by chance (Supplementary Information S1 lists both difference sets).

The 52 months carry 104 poles. Of these, 61 poles (58.7 percent) fall inside the CONUS land area, 27 (26.0 percent) in Canada, and 16 (15.4 percent) in Mexico, so 41.3 percent of the catalog poles are anchored outside the United States. The dry poles sit outside the CONUS in 44.2 percent of months and the wet poles in 38.5 percent, the dry poles concentrate along the northern edge of the domain, with 20 dry poles at or north of 47.75 N, and the wet poles are more widely scattered (Figure 2). The 15 US-anchored months, listed in the Supplementary Information, are the months in which the dipole is anchored at both ends inside the country whose hazards this paper counts.

Of the 672 displayed cells, 229 have Poisson intervals excluding 1, with 164 above 1 and 65 below 1, and the report-weighted aggregate over the displayed cells is 1.06. The pattern is elevation across the Midwest and the Ohio and Tennessee valleys, with deficits on the southern High Plains. Because the intervals are unadjusted per-cell intervals and neighboring cells are not independent, the map is a diagnostic display of where CDPE-month reports concentrate, not a test of local effects, and we draw no local inference from it.

## 3.2 Headline conditioning

Over 1980 to 2023 the domain recorded 54,607 tornado reports (25,533 rated F/EF1 or stronger and 7,958 rated F/EF2 or stronger) and 379,430 hail reports, of which 229,650 were at least 1.00 inch (Figure 1, panel a). At the aligned catalog, same-month hazard occurrence relative to calendar-matched non-CDPE months was close to one for all three hazards under both estimators (Table 2, Figure 3). The stratified ratios are 1.16 (0.78 to 1.53) for tornadoes, 1.01 (0.70 to 1.37) for severe hail, and 0.88 (0.50 to 1.42) for heat-wave days. The primary NB estimates are 1.09 (0.79 to 1.49) for tornadoes, 1.12 (0.89 to 1.41) for severe hail, and 0.91 (0.60 to 1.37) for heat-wave days. On the US-anchored subset the stratified ratios are 0.99, 1.00, and 0.93, and the NB estimates are 1.04 (0.53 to 2.02), 0.98 (0.74 to 1.29), and 1.05 (0.59 to 1.89), with the wider intervals the 15-month subset implies. ## 3.3 The lagged arm

The lagged arm moves the convective estimates toward one and leaves heat-wave days above one (Table 2). For the aligned catalog, the lagged stratified ratios are 1.12 (0.84 to 1.44) for tornadoes, 1.10 (0.76 to 1.49) for severe hail, and 1.15 (0.56 to 2.17) for heat-wave days. The lagged NB estimates are 1.00 (0.79 to 1.25) for tornadoes and 0.96 (0.79 to 1.17) for severe hail, against same-month values of 1.09 and 1.12, while the heat-wave estimate rises from 0.91 to 1.14 (0.66 to 1.96). On the US-anchored subset the lagged heat-wave ratio is 0.60 (0.32 to 1.04) with an NB estimate of 0.73 (0.40 to 1.32), the lowest headline estimate in the paper, and its interval still includes 1. The paired comparison of the two arms, a month-paired bootstrap that refits both count models on the same resampled outcome months, gives differences in log incidence rate ratio of 0.09 (-0.22 to 0.37) for tornadoes, 0.15 (-0.16 to 0.46) for severe hail, and -0.22 (-0.85 to 0.47) for heat-wave days on the aligned catalog. Every paired interval includes zero, so the movements between arms are not distinguishable from estimation noise, and Section 4 reports the two arms without a causal reading of their difference.

## 3.4 Robustness

Excluding the April and May 2011 CDPE months lowers every same-month ratio and brings the lagged ratios to one (Figure 4). The same-month stratified ratios become 1.00 (0.73 to 1.30) for tornadoes, 0.86 (0.67 to 1.04) for severe hail, and 0.70 (0.42 to 1.13) for heat-wave days, and the lagged ratios become 0.98, 0.98, and 1.00. The NB analogues move the same way: same-month estimates of 1.01 (0.72 to 1.41), 1.08 (0.85 to 1.36), and 0.80 (0.54 to 1.20), and lagged estimates of 0.92 (0.76 to 1.11), 0.92 (0.78 to 1.08), and 1.02 (0.63 to 1.68). No single month carries the headline estimates either: leave-one-month-out ranges for the same-month aligned ratios are 0.99 to 1.20 for tornadoes, 0.92 to 1.05 for severe hail, and 0.76 to 0.92 for heat-wave days, and the full ranges and the median contrasts are tabulated in the Supplementary Information.

The split-era display gives within-era ratios of 0.90 in 1980 to 1995 and 1.33 in 1996 to 2023 for tornadoes, 0.77 (0.60 to 0.98) and 1.08 for severe hail, and 0.75 and 0.99 for heat-wave days. The era differences are 1.47 (0.85 to 2.24) for tornadoes, 1.41 (0.98 to 2.01) for severe hail, and 1.32 (0.46 to 3.28) for heat-wave days. Every era-difference interval includes 1, so the era differences are compatible with noise, and we make no claim that any association emerged or grew between eras. The full split-era table, including the lagged arm, is in the Supplementary Information.

## 3.5 Pole-relative strata

The one structured pattern in the paper is geographic, and it is confined to the regime month itself (Table 3). In CDPE months, tornado reports in cells near the wet pole occur at 2.52 (1.12 to 5.02) times the matched-control rate, reports near the dry pole at 0.56 (0.17 to 0.77), and reports elsewhere at 1.16 (0.87 to 1.55). For severe hail the corresponding ratios are 1.13 (0.69 to 1.74) near the wet pole, 0.71 (0.28 to 1.02) near the dry pole, and 1.04 (0.78 to 1.35) elsewhere. The strata are small shares of the whole: the near-dry and near-wet strata hold 1.4 percent and 6.3 percent of CDPE-month tornado reports and 2.2 percent and 4.1 percent of CDPE-month severe-hail reports, so the pole-relative estimates describe a small part of each hazard total and carry the wide intervals the counts imply.

The lagged strata do not reproduce that pattern. Counting the following month's reports against the same poles, the tornado ratios are 1.37 (0.83 to 1.92) near the wet pole, 1.40 (0.91 to 2.17) near the dry pole, and 1.13 (0.86 to 1.48) elsewhere, and the severe-hail ratios are 1.19 (0.61 to 1.94), 0.84 (0.37 to 1.52), and 1.14 (0.88 to 1.44). Every lagged interval includes 1, and the same-month near-dry tornado deficit inverts. The pole-relative family comprises 12 stratum-hazard estimates, six per timing, with no multiplicity adjustment, and the same-month pattern carries the circularity of Section 2.3 in geographic form: a wet pole is located where the month's rain was heaviest and convective storms produce rain, while a dry pole is located where little rain fell, so few same-month storms near a dry pole is close to a restatement of the classification. We therefore report the same-month pole-relative estimates as exploratory, and Section 4 draws the conclusion the lagged comparison supports.

## 3.6 Definition envelope and corrected sensitivity arms

Across the 23 included Arm A configurations, hazard rate ratios run from 0.99 to 1.50 for tornadoes, from 0.86 to 1.53 for severe hail, and from 0.56 to 2.62 for heat-wave days (Figure 3). The counts behind the envelope vary steeply with the detector settings (Figure 5), and the corrected variance decomposition puts the detection threshold first in both arms on the log-count scale, with shares of 0.854 in Arm A and 0.786 in Arm B, and the radius second, with shares of 0.076 and 0.120 (Figure 6). The configurations are nested and share months and hazard series, so these ranges describe the spread of one configuration family, not the spread of independent estimates.

The hail envelope is the clearest illustration in the paper of why the envelope matters. Exactly 7 of the 23 included hail configurations have intervals excluding 1, and all seven are SPEI-3 or SPEI-6 configurations, the multi-month accumulation definitions, not members of the SPEI-1 baseline family (the seven are listed in the Supplementary Information). A single-catalog study built on one of those definitions would have reported a hail elevation that the baseline family does not support.

Arm B changes materially once its detector artifact is corrected. The original detector resolved ties among exactly equal pole cells by first occurrence, which parks capped empirical-transform poles at the southernmost tied cell, where the coastal missing-data gate then rejects most months. With the deterministic tie-break of the Supplementary Information in place, the number of zero-count scenarios falls from 272 of 720 to 58 of 720, the masked NARR SPEI-6 empirical baseline count is 68 months rather than 6, and the fitting-distribution share of log-count variance in Arm B collapses from 0.345 before the fix to 0.0003 after it (on raw counts, from 0.188 to 0.005). The distribution sensitivity in the original Arm B was therefore substantially a tie-breaking artifact, and the corrected ordering, threshold first and radius second, matches Arm A. The land-mask comparison is a caution in the same spirit: applying a land mask to computed fields changes the field the detector sees, including removing the northern-edge dry cells that the unmasked fields can anchor on, and in the original run it reduced the NARR and ERA5 baseline counts from 175 and 217 months to 44 and 69. We report it as a warning about comparing catalogs across masks, not as a finding about land-only dipoles.

## 3.7 Detectability and remaining diagnostics

The detectability limits bound every estimate above. At 80 percent power, the minimum detectable rate ratios for the same-month aligned design are 1.67 for tornadoes, 1.74 for severe hail, and 1.87 for heat-wave days at n = 52, and 2.05, 2.11, and 2.40 on the US-anchored subset at n = 15. For the lagged aligned design they are 1.61, 1.65, and 2.21. The full MDES table is in the Supplementary Information. Annual CDPE month counts are essentially uncorrelated with annual hazard totals: Spearman correlations of -0.14 (p = 0.38) for tornadoes, -0.20 (p = 0.19) for severe hail, and 0.02 (p = 0.91) for heat-wave days over the 44 years. The aligned catalog also skews early: 24 of its 52 months (46.2 percent) fall in 1980 to 1995, against that era share of 36.4 percent of the window, a skew the era stratification of Figure 1 (panel b) and the split-era display of Section 3.4 are built to absorb.

# 4. Discussion

The frequency evidence is inconclusive, and the detectability analysis says why that is the honest summary. The headline intervals include rate ratios of roughly 1.4 to 1.5 for the convective hazards, and the design detects only rate ratios above about 1.6 to 1.9 at the aligned sample size, so the estimates bound the association rather than excluding it. A stronger design, meaning a longer record, a larger catalog, or a sharper estimand, would be needed to separate an association of plausible size from one, and this paper does not claim a null.

One era-restricted estimate needs explicit treatment, because it is the only split-era interval in the paper that excludes 1. The within-era severe-hail ratio for 1980 to 1995 is 0.77 (0.60 to 0.98), against 1.08 in 1996 to 2023. Read on its own it would describe an early-era hail deficit in CDPE months that the later era does not share. Three considerations argue against building on it. The era difference is 1.41 (0.98 to 2.01), which includes 1, so the two eras are compatible with a common value, and the pooled stratified estimate of 1.01 sits between them. The split-era display reports six era contrasts in the main table across the hazards, more in the Supplementary Information, without multiplicity adjustment, so one interval excluding 1 is within what such a display produces by chance. And hail is the least stationary of the three series: the early era predates the completed NEXRAD deployment and the mature spotter networks, and reporting inhomogeneity is the documented weak point of the hail record. We leave the early-era value in the table and in this paragraph, and we rest no conclusion on it.

The lagged arm repeats the estimation with the outcome month following the index month, which removes the channel by which storms contribute precipitation to their own regime classification. The lagged NB estimates differ from the same-month estimates (tornadoes 1.00 against 1.09, severe hail 0.96 against 1.12, heat-wave days 1.14 against 0.91), but the paired differences are small against their intervals: the month-paired bootstrap of Section 3.3 gives differences in log incidence rate ratio of 0.09 (-0.22 to 0.37), 0.15 (-0.16 to 0.46), and -0.22 (-0.85 to 0.47), all including zero. The two arms are therefore not distinguishable in these data, and we draw no conclusion from their difference in either direction: the same-month estimates are not shown to be reverse-caused, and the lagged estimates are not shown to be the cleaner values. The arms also answer different physical questions, concurrence against one-month persistence, which is a further reason to report both without ranking them.

The pole-relative pattern is the one structured geographic result in the paper, and the lagged arm decides how it should be read. Same-month, tornado reports concentrate near the wet pole at 2.52 times the matched rate and thin near the dry pole at 0.56. A wet pole is by construction where the month's precipitation was heaviest, convective storms are precipitation producers, and a dry pole is where little precipitation fell, so reverse causation predicts this geometry on its own, with no circulation mechanism required. When reports are counted in the following month, with the poles fixed by the regime month, the pattern is gone: the near-wet tornado estimate is 1.37 (0.83 to 1.92) and the near-dry estimate inverts to 1.40 (0.91 to 2.17). We therefore report the same-month pattern as exploratory, a description of how reports and index poles coincide within a month, and not as evidence that CDPE regimes direct storms toward the wet pole. A physical test of that idea has to compare the environments of events directly rather than their coincidence with index poles, which is the agenda of a companion physical-dynamics analysis. The strata also hold small shares of each hazard total (1.4 percent near the dry pole and 6.3 percent near the wet pole, of CDPE-month tornado reports), so even as description the pattern covers a small part of the hazard burden.

The envelope results carry the methodological conclusion. Hazard associations estimated on regime catalogs are only as stable as the catalogs, and here the hail result makes the point concretely: the seven configurations whose intervals exclude 1 are all multi-month accumulation definitions, so a definitional choice that a single-catalog paper would rarely flag decides its headline inference. Reporting the envelope, the provenance of each analysis set, and the detectability limits beside the headline estimate is the minimum standard these results support, and it is the standard this paper applies to itself, including in the corrected Arm B, where a detector tie-breaking rule, not a climate property, produced most of the original distribution sensitivity.

Six limitations bound the results. First, the SPEI-3 and SPEI-6 envelope rests on SPEIbase v2.10, because v2.11 files for those timescales could not be obtained, so no aligned multi-month catalog exists and the lagged arm is a SPEI-1 arm only. Second, 41.3 percent of the aligned catalog poles fall outside the CONUS, which motivates the US-anchored subset, whose 15 months widen every interval it carries. Third, Storm Events reporting is non-stationary, and calendar matching and era displays absorb only part of that inhomogeneity. Fourth, the catalog skews toward the earlier era, with 46.2 percent of months in 1980 to 1995. Fifth, the pole-relative strata hold small shares of each hazard total, so the exploratory pole-relative pattern rests on small counts. Sixth, Arm B ends in 2014, because the reanalysis factorial was built on the 1980 to 2014 window, so its sensitivity results describe that window only.

# 5. Conclusions

Conditioning tornadoes, severe hail, and heat waves on the shared CDPE catalog returns no decisive frequency association. Same-month estimates sit near one with intervals that include effects of substantive size, the lagged arm returns convective estimates of 1.00 and 0.96 with paired differences from the same-month estimates that include zero, and the design detects only rate ratios above about 1.6 to 1.9, so the correct conclusion is inconclusive at this design, not a proven null. Two results stand beside that summary. Tornado reports coincide geographically with the index poles within CDPE months, concentrated near the wet pole and reduced near the dry pole, but that pattern does not survive lagging and is reported as exploratory. And the sensitivity arms show that catalog choices, down to a detector tie-breaking rule, can decide what a regime-conditioning study reports, which is why this paper reports its estimates with their provenance, envelope, and detectability limits attached.
